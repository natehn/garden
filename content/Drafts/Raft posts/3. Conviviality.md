# the role of host

- For decades, we have used the term “fiscal sponsor,” but the term Open Collective coined in the late 2010’s, fiscal host seems far more apt. And it foregrounds the question: how might we fiscal sponsors, [become better hosts](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14314/pg14314-images.html)?
- - Even better, for a place that holds a gathering, or a party, we can call it a “hub.” And it is more than just fiscal - it is operational. So, we can call it an operational hub - almost like a live-work home or hostel or b&b for FSPs, travelers there for an extended stay. We want travelers that will choose to stay and make the place worth staying for others who are seeking community.
    - We must consider, as Social Impact Commons encourages, fiscal sponsors as commons. At Open Collective Foundation, our marketing talked about the organization as a legal and financial (and by extension through OCI, technological) commons. While our messaging was ahead of our reality, I still believe this should be the goal of any radical fiscal sponsor.
- A good host is paying close attention to the well-being of guests
- - Supporting them to be their best selves - introducing them to funders, going to them and helping them with grant applications, and having fundraising support on call
    - Supporting them when they experience conflict, and having appropriate (i.e., one tech-coded and one social-justice-coded) conflict facilitators on call
- Serving the conviviality of “attendees,” not the government

**Tech is a tool, our data is ours:** We understand that "open source" is no panacea, and is in fact the bedrock of post-dotcom technocapitalism. We are thus quite interested in experiments and more radical licenses. Fundamentally, however, we a practical approach to technology, prioritizing usability/accessibility, then data sovereignty, then business/ownership/stewardship/governance structure, then protocols and interoperability, then licensing and self-hostability.

FAIR principles

0. On interoperability

We seek to build systems that work. And part of having systems that work is that they can speak to one another. We push to create efficiencies so that we can work smarter, not harder. In the end, our goal is to work less, and live more - not through the largess of AI corporate overlords, but through the bounty of our collective wits.

1. On open source

We believe that the open source ecosystem has a place in the liberatory future we want. But we also acknowledge that in the capitalist present, the libertarian, anti-corporate politics of libre software has been co-opted by Silicon Valley, allowing them to leverage cheap and free labor to more easily build their technocracy.

We are thus interested in supporting the proliferation of copyfair (or commons-based reciprocity) licenses that keep technology from being co-opted by hyper-capitalist interests. But given the choice between a proprietary app and a fully viable open source alternative, we will always choose open source.

In some cases - as with helpdesk software, for example - there is not a suitable (and interoperable) open source alternative, and in these cases, we try to use the best available software, with the best nonprofit discount, that helps us accomplish our goals and does not push us to act in ways that are misaligned with the future we seek.

2. On self-reliance

None of us can survive alone; but wherever the opportunity arises, as a community we seek to rely on our own technical prowess. We self-host our technology as much as we can, using hosting providers that will do their best to protect us, away from prying eyes. We also hope to create an environment where there is the opportunity for community members to learn and grow, as they take on more and more responsibility for the systems’ maintenance.

3. On nontechnical accessibility

We create systems that are as usable as possible for people with lower access to technology. Email is still fundamental to how we relate to our people. And wherever a more technological solution is required, we will create ample documentation, in all necessary languages, to help guide community members along the path.

For tools that will need to be managed by nontechnical staff members on a regular basis (for example, a marketing website) the accessibility of the interface will be a greater priority.

4. On decentralization

Fiscal sponsorship is essentially a practice in centralization, where we combine our resources in order to create a shared commons and take advantage of economies of scale. At the same time, this centralization of administration and start-up costs can enable greater autonomy and nimbleness for projects, lending is some of the flavor of a more decentralized network.

We leverage the tenets of decentralization wherever we can, distributing decisionmaking, using P2P, torrents, and other decentralized technologies, and by creating redundancy wherever we can.
