> When I hear a lecture on handwriting teaching or technique, the speaker sometimes comments at the end about left-handed writers. The speaker, nearly always right-handed, says something like, ‘. . . and the opposite way round for left-handers, of course.’ But what is ‘the opposite war round’?
> 
> Because most writing teachers are right-handed, they demonstrate the correct movements and expect the learner to make his own adaptation. In many situations this is a reasonable assumption, as we left-handers are alwas having to adapt our movements from those of right-handed teachers of physical skills. If you are learning a tennis forehand, for example, the right-handed teacher stands with his left side toward the net, strokes with his right arm, and, as he does so, transfers his weight from his right to his left leg. All those lefts and rights must be reversed by the left-handed learner, but it is a fairly simple exercise because everything is exactly reversible. That is, the left-hander can perform the mirror-image of his teacher’s movements; the only difference between the left- and right-handed tennis player is that one’s forehand side is the other’s backhand side, but the strokes are the same. 
> 
> Handwriting, I submit, is an entirely different kind of skill, because the writing has to go from left to right. If the left-hander were free to write from right to left, or even if we all wrote boustrophedon, then it would be analogous to a tennis stroke. But there is no way in which the right-hander’s backhand, pulling motion can be duplicated by the left-hander, who must *push* the pen in one way or another; he starts from the left and moves right instead of starting from the right and moving right. Most left-handed writers whom I have observed (while invigilating examinations) keep their writing hands below the line; some, myself included, write from an above-the-line position. In writing from below the line they copy the hand-position of their right-handed teachers as nearly as possible, but, if they use fountain pens, they will need oblique nibs, since the axis of their pens will be about 90° to the left of the right-hander. By writing from above the line, others of us imitate the angle of the right-hander’s pen - that is, we have moved around still another 90° to be 180° from the position of the right-hander. ([Footnote:] There is a good deal of variation here; my own angle is more like 140°. Some writers appear to move beyond 180° to a position something like 90° to the *right* of a right-hander’s pen. These angles are all relative and very approximate, of course, because right-handed writers hold their pens at varying angles, too.) We push where he pulls, pull where he pushes, but we do not need oblique nibs. I have heard some commentators on handwriting claim that this way of holding the pen is wrong and is a result of allowing the child to grope towards his own method without giving him proper guidance. Rather, I think, it shows up the problem of the left-hander trying to imitate his teacher: does he copy the teacher’s movements by keeping his hand below the line as the teacher does, or does he copy the teacher’s drawn line by recreating it in reverse and therefore hanving to hold the hand above the line with his pen at a 180° angle to the teacher’s? (This is not to overlook the other reason we write above the line, which is to keep our hands out of the fresh ink.)
> 
> Right-handed teachers of handwriting assume that left-handed students should hold their pens just as they, the teachers, do, and that the relationships and angles between writer, pen, paper, and writing surfaces should all be more or less the same as for right-handed writers. The problem facing right-handed teachers of any manual teachers is to decide to what extent a left-handed person should be taught the mirror-image of one’s own movements and how far he should develop his own, possibly more natural, movements. When the movement is perfectly symmetrical, like the tennis forehand, the learner can make the necessary left/right substitutions and reproduce the movement on his left. But when the movement is asymmetrical, like handwriting (I can’t think of a single other example), this assumption about doing it ‘the opposite way round’, as though there were only *one* ‘opposite way round’, does not work.

“Writing Left-Handed,” by Sarah Lawson, in the *The Jᴏᴜʀɴᴀʟ of the Society for Italic Handwriting*, Number 92, Autumn 1977
