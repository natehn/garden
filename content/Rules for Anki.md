1. I use quality information. ("Garbage in, garbage out.")
2. I keep all my cards in one deck, unless I'm cramming something specific and short-term.
3. I [write my own cards](https://medium.com/@jhvithamar/making-memory-a-choice-7-tips-for-getting-started-with-anki-4fd138aee3a8).
4. I default to bi-directional cards for the most part (thus, neither side has a question mark).
5. I create many cards from a single "note" (by leveraging fields).
6. I keep information concise and as [atomic](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zFgK9ArxAXq57iMukRsVVE9) as possible.[^boom] In Andy's words, they should be [focused, precise, tractable (easy-ish), require memory recall (not problem solving/inference), and produce a consistent answer](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/). Vague cards get vague results.
7. I write cards that [emphasize facts' relationships](https://leananki.com/learn-complex-information/) and that provide the [interesting explanations](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/) hiding behind facts.
8. Where applicable, I use etymology prompts to add a layer of understanding above definition-learning.
9. I include an "extra" field with answers for optional additional information and context.
10. I'm not afraid to include things I need to remember from my personal life.

[^boom]: Boom!

Something I'm still working on is adding mnemonics to work (like the carrot example [here](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/))
